**Quick answer:** Foreigners can buy property in Norway with almost no restrictions. Expect a minimum 15% deposit for a home you'll live in (often 35%+ for a pure investment or secondary property), mortgage rates around 4–5%, and gross rental yields of roughly 3–4% in Oslo. The market is stable and transparent, but the financing rules are among the strictest in Europe.

Norway is one of the most stable, transparent property markets in Europe — with among the strictest financing rules in the developed world. This guide is for foreign investors trying to figure out whether Norway makes sense for their portfolio, and if so, how to actually get in.

## Can foreigners buy property in Norway?

Yes, with almost no restrictions. Unlike Denmark, Norway does not require government permission for most residential purchases. EU/EEA citizens can buy freely. Non-EEA citizens can buy freely for residential use — restrictions only apply to agricultural land and certain concession properties in specific rural municipalities.

**What you can't easily get is a mortgage.** That's the real gate.

## What the Norwegian market actually is

Norway is a **capital-preservation market with modest yields, not a cash-flow market**. Set expectations correctly:

- Gross rental yields in Oslo: **3.2-4.5 %**
- Gross yields in Bergen, Trondheim, Stavanger: **4.5-6 %**
- Gross yields in Tromsø, Kristiansand, and secondary cities: **5-7 %**
- Long-run appreciation across Norway: **3-5 % annually**, with high volatility
- Currency risk (NOK) is real — the krone can swing 15-25 % against EUR/USD over a 3-year window

If your target is monthly cash flow from day one, Norway is not the right market. If you're building a diversified Nordic portfolio and want a stable, currency-diversified allocation with long-run appreciation, Norway earns its place.

## Financing as a foreign buyer

Norwegian banks apply strict rules to all mortgage lending — foreign or domestic:

- **Maximum LTV**: 85 % (Norway-wide) or 60 % (secondary property that isn't your primary residence)
- **Debt-to-income cap**: total debt ≤ 5x annual gross income
- **Rate stress test**: you must be able to service the loan at rates 3 percentage points above the offer rate
- **Payment-to-income**: monthly debt service ≤ 40 % of gross monthly income

For an investment property (i.e., not your primary home), the **60 % LTV cap is the hard one** — you need at minimum 40 % down in cash. On a 4.5 M NOK Oslo apartment that's 1.8 M NOK (~150,000 EUR) before transaction costs.

**Foreign-buyer-specific hurdles:**

- Norwegian banks strongly prefer borrowers with Norwegian income or an established relationship (deposit account for 12+ months minimum)
- Non-resident foreigners without Norwegian income often can't get a mortgage at all — you'll need cash-buy or financing from a bank in your home country
- Even EU citizens with Norwegian residence permit need proof of employment or independent income

**Practical implication:** Unless you plan to relocate to Norway or already have Norwegian income, budget for **100 % cash purchase** or accept that you'll finance in your home country with the associated currency mismatch.

## Taxes as a non-resident owner

Norway taxes rental income and capital gains regardless of your residency status. The 2026 headline rates:

- **Rental income**: 22 % flat tax (net income after all deductions)
- **Capital gain at sale**: 22 % on the gain
- **Wealth tax**: 0.85 % annually on net wealth above 1.7 M NOK (yes, real estate counts; yes, non-residents pay it on Norwegian assets)
- **Property tax (eiendomsskatt)**: municipal, 0.1-0.7 % annually depending on kommune

**Double-taxation treaty:** Norway has treaties with most EU countries, the UK, US, and many others. You typically get a credit in your home country for taxes paid in Norway, but details vary. Consult a cross-border tax advisor before completing the purchase — the structure decision has 5-figure annual consequences.

## Transaction costs

Norwegian transaction costs are lower than most European markets but not zero:

- **Dokumentavgift (document tax)**: 2.5 % of property value (paid by buyer)
- **Tinglysningsgebyr**: ~525 NOK per document
- **Real estate agent**: paid by seller
- **Legal**: 10,000-25,000 NOK for a straightforward purchase

Budget **3-3.5 % of purchase price** for transaction costs on top of the down payment.

## Realistic strategy for foreign investors

1. **Confirm eligibility and financing before you shop.** The cash-vs-mortgage question dominates every subsequent decision.
2. **Pick your driver deliberately.** In Norway, the driver is almost always appreciation + currency diversification, not cash flow. If you need monthly income, Norway is the wrong market.
3. **Choose the right city for your risk tolerance.** Central Oslo = safest, lowest yield. Trondheim/Bergen = balanced. Stavanger = tied to oil sector volatility. Tromsø = tourism upside, weather risk.
4. **Model at +3 percentage points.** Norwegian rules already stress-test this — model it yourself before you bid.
5. **Plan a 7+ year hold.** Transaction costs (~3.5 %) plus the appreciation-driven return profile both reward patience.
6. **Get a Norwegian accountant or a cross-border firm.** Wealth tax + double-taxation treaty administration is not something to DIY.
