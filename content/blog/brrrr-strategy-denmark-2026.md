## What is BRRRR?

BRRRR stands for **Buy, Rehab, Rent, Refinance, Repeat**. It's a real estate strategy pioneered in the US by investors like David Greene and Brandon Turner, designed to recycle the same capital across multiple properties instead of saving a new down payment for each one.

The cycle:

1. **Buy** an undervalued property below market
2. **Rehab** it to force appreciation
3. **Rent** it at the new market rate
4. **Refinance** based on the higher After-Repair Value (ARV) to pull most of your capital back out
5. **Repeat** on the next deal with the same money

BRRRR works exceptionally well in the US where investors can cash-out refinance at 75% LTV of ARV. **In Denmark it works differently** because of realkredit rules, but it still works — and this guide shows how to adapt it.

## The Danish adaptation

Denmark doesn't have "cash-out refinancing" the way the US does. Instead you have:

- **Realkredit lån**: mortgage bonds up to 80% of the property's current valuation, 30-year terms, historically among the cheapest financing in Europe.
- **Bank tillægslån** (top-up loan): secured lending up to a further 15% of value, higher interest, shorter term. Rare in practice for investment property.
- **New valuation at refinance**: after renovation, realkreditinstituttet re-values the property. If the ARV is higher, you can refinance at 80% of the new valuation and pull the equity out.

So the Danish BRRRR looks like this:

1. Buy at price P₀ with 20% down (kontant) + 80% realkredit
2. Renovate for cost R (usually cash or short-term bank loan)
3. Rent at new market rate
4. Get the property revalued (new appraisal): ARV = P₁
5. Refinance to 80% of P₁ — this releases (0.8 × P₁ − 0.8 × P₀) in capital

The math only works if the renovation adds more value than it costs. That's the entire game.

## The 70% rule adapted for Denmark

In the US, investors use the 70% rule: **buy at (0.70 × ARV) − renovation cost**.

In Denmark, given 80% realkredit LTV and typical transaction costs of 3–5%, the adapted formula is:

**Max buy price = (0.75 × ARV) − renovation cost**

If you can't buy at or below this number, the refinance won't return your capital.

Example: A property with ARV of 3,000,000 DKK and expected renovation of 400,000 DKK should not be bought above (0.75 × 3,000,000) − 400,000 = **1,850,000 DKK**.

At that purchase price:
- Down payment (20%): 370,000 DKK
- Renovation: 400,000 DKK
- Total cash in: 770,000 DKK

After refinance at 80% of 3,000,000 DKK ARV:
- New loan: 2,400,000 DKK
- Original loan payoff: 1,480,000 DKK
- Cash out: 920,000 DKK

You've deployed 770,000 DKK, pulled 920,000 DKK back out, and still own the property with all future cash flow and appreciation. That's a full BRRRR.

## Where BRRRR works in Denmark

The strategy needs three conditions:

1. **Below-market purchase price** — usually because the property needs work, has an odd layout, or the seller is motivated
2. **Renovation upside** — the market rewards the specific upgrades you're making (kitchen, bath, layout, energy rating)
3. **Rental demand** at the ARV rent level

Best areas (2026):

| City | Why BRRRR works | Typical margin |
|---|---|---|
| Aalborg | Student demand, older stock, active rehab market | 15–25% ARV lift |
| Aarhus (except centrum) | Suburb pockets undervalued, rehab premium | 10–20% |
| Odense C | Cheap m² prices, SDU rental base | 15–25% |
| Esbjerg, Fredericia, Kolding | Lowest entry prices, room to force value | 20–35% |
| Herning, Holstebro | Rural cash flow BRRRR, harder exit | 15–30% |
| Copenhagen | BRRRR is nearly impossible — priced too tight | 0–8% |

Copenhagen is a *bad* BRRRR market because the entry price already reflects renovation upside. You pay for the ARV before you do the work. Danish BRRRR happens in secondary cities.

## The three ways BRRRR fails

**1. Over-renovation.** Spending 500,000 DKK to lift a 1,800,000 DKK property to 2,100,000 DKK is a 200,000 DKK loss. The renovation must return **more than 1× its cost in appraised value**, or the refinance releases less capital than you spent.

**2. Bad valuation.** The realkredit institution's appraiser doesn't have to agree with your ARV estimate. Always run comparables through official DST EJ55 per-m² data for the exact postcode — not annonce estimates.

**3. Interest rate change during the hold.** If rates rise 2 percentage points between purchase and refinance, the refinance still works (you're getting more equity out) but the ongoing cash flow gets crushed. Stress-test every deal at +2 percentage points.

## The tax angle

Renovation costs on an investment property in Denmark fall into two categories:

- **Vedligeholdelse** (maintenance): fully deductible in the tax year — replacing broken windows, patching a roof, repairing kitchen wear
- **Forbedring** (improvement): capitalized to the property's cost basis, deducted against future sales gain — a new kitchen, added bathroom, layout change

For a BRRRR deal, most of the renovation is *forbedring* — it forces value, which by definition means improvement, not maintenance. That means you don't get the tax deduction in year one; you get it when you eventually sell.

If you hold in **virksomhedsordningen** or an **ApS**, the treatment is more favorable. Consult an accountant before your first BRRRR — the structure decision has 5-figure consequences over the life of the property.

## Modeling a full BRRRR cycle

The BRRRR math is more complex than a normal buy-and-hold analysis. You need to model:

- Purchase price + all transaction costs
- Renovation cost + timing
- Bridge financing for the rehab (if any)
- ARV (independent estimate, not seller's number)
- New rental income at market rate
- Refinance loan amount at 80% of ARV
- Cash recovered vs cash left in the deal
- Post-refinance cash flow and cash-on-cash return
- Return on the cash *actually left in the deal* (which for a good BRRRR should be near-infinite)

1. Pick one secondary city and learn its per-m² prices cold (Aalborg or Odense are the easiest)
