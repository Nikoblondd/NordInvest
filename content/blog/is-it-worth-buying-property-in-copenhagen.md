## The Short Answer

Copenhagen can absolutely be worth buying into — but almost never as a cash flow play. It is an **appreciation market**. If you understand that going in, it is one of the most stable, liquid property markets in Europe. If you buy expecting fat monthly profits from rent, you will be disappointed.

This guide walks through how to judge a specific Copenhagen deal.

## Why Yields Are Low Here

Central Copenhagen apartments typically trade at gross yields around 3–4%. That is low by international standards, and there is a straightforward reason: demand is high, supply is constrained, and the market is considered safe. Safe, liquid assets command premium prices, which compresses yield.

In other words, the low yield is a *symptom of strength*, not weakness. The same dynamic exists in cities like Munich, Zurich, and Stockholm.

What it means practically: at current price levels, a typical leveraged Copenhagen apartment often runs close to break-even or slightly negative on monthly cash flow. The return has to come from somewhere else.

## The Appreciation Case

The argument for Copenhagen rests on long-term price growth and capital preservation:

- **Constrained supply.** Limited room to build in desirable central districts keeps upward pressure on prices.
- **Strong fundamentals.** A wealthy, growing city with high employment and steady population inflow.
- **Liquidity.** Quality apartments in good areas sell. Your capital is not trapped.

If an area appreciates even 3–4% per year, a leveraged buyer's *equity* return can be substantial — because the growth applies to the whole property value, not just the cash you put in. That is the engine that makes a low-yield, slightly-negative-cash-flow Copenhagen apartment work.

## The Risks You Must Price In

Being honest about the downside is what separates investors from speculators:

- **Interest rate sensitivity.** A break-even property at today's rates can turn sharply negative if rates rise. Always stress-test.
- **High entry costs.** Transaction taxes and fees mean you need a multi-year hold to come out ahead.
- **Concentration.** A single expensive apartment is a large, undiversified bet.
- **Appreciation is not guaranteed.** The long-term trend has been up, but there have been flat and declining stretches. Your hold period must be long enough to ride them out.

## How to Judge a Specific Deal

Run any Copenhagen property through these four questions:

1. **What is the net yield?** Below ~3%, you are buying pure appreciation. Make sure the area justifies it.
2. **What is the monthly cash flow after a mortgage?** Expect break-even or slightly negative — but quantify exactly how negative, and whether you can comfortably fund it.
3. **Does it survive a +2% interest rate shock?** If a small rate rise sinks you, the deal is too tight.
4. **Is the area genuinely appreciating?** Use per-square-metre price history for that specific district — not a national average.

If the answers are "low yield, mildly negative but fundable cash flow, survives a rate shock, strong appreciating district" — that is a textbook Copenhagen appreciation asset and likely worth buying.

If it is "low yield, deeply negative cash flow, fails the rate shock, flat district" — walk away, however nice the apartment.
