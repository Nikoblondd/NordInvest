## The 60-Second Answer

A rental property is a good deal when **gross yield ≥ 5%**, **monthly cash flow is positive after a stress test**, and **cash-on-cash ROI ≥ 8%** — all measured against the local market benchmark. If any one of those three fails, the deal needs a specific reason (renovation upside, exceptional location, value-add potential) to still make sense. Below is how to check each one in under a minute.

## The 5 Checks That Decide a Rental Deal

You don't need a spreadsheet, a mentor, or a course. You need five numbers. If a rental clears all five, it's a good deal. If it fails one, it might still work — but only with a reason. If it fails two or more, walk away.

1. **Gross rental yield** — is the rent-to-price ratio high enough?
2. **Monthly cash flow** — does the property pay for itself?
3. **Cash-on-cash ROI** — what does your invested money actually earn?
4. **Break-even rent** — how much margin do you have if a tenant leaves?
5. **Local benchmark** — how does it compare to similar properties in the same area?

Let's work through each in turn, with a Copenhagen example.

## Check 1 — Gross Rental Yield (10 seconds)

Gross yield is the fastest filter. It tells you whether a property is even in the right ballpark before you do any deeper math.

**Formula:** Gross Yield = (Monthly Rent × 12 ÷ Price) × 100

**Benchmark:**

- **Below 4%** — appreciation play only, expect negative cash flow
- **4–5%** — borderline, needs strong cash management
- **5–6%** — solid cashflow market
- **6–8%** — strong cashflow deal
- **Above 8%** — either a steal, a typo, or something is wrong

**Example:** A €500,000 Copenhagen apartment renting for €1,800/month.
Annual rent = €21,600. Gross yield = €21,600 ÷ €500,000 × 100 = **4.32%**.

That's borderline. Central Copenhagen is structurally a 3–4.5% market, so this is at the top of the range — meaning it's priced reasonably for the city, but won't generate strong cash flow.

## Check 2 — Monthly Cash Flow (15 seconds)

Yield doesn't pay your mortgage. Cash flow does.

**Formula:** Monthly Cash Flow = Monthly Rent − Monthly Mortgage − Monthly Expenses

**Benchmark:**

- **Above €300/month positive** — strong
- **€0 to €300/month positive** — acceptable
- **€-200 to €0/month negative** — only OK in high-appreciation markets
- **Below €-200/month negative** — appreciation must be exceptional to justify

**Example, continued:**
20% down (€100,000), 4.5% mortgage rate, 30-year amortization → monthly payment ≈ €2,027.
Plus €300/month expenses (insurance, taxes, maintenance reserve).

Monthly cash flow = €1,800 − €2,027 − €300 = **−€527/month**.

That's a hard fail on cash flow. The deal only works if Copenhagen appreciation continues at 4–6% CAGR for long enough to outrun the negative cash flow. That's a real bet, but it's a bet — not a free lunch.

## Check 3 — Cash-on-Cash ROI (15 seconds)

ROI tells you what your *invested money* earns each year — the number that lets you compare a rental to a stock, a bond, or a bank account.

**Formula:** Cash-on-Cash ROI = (Annual Cash Flow ÷ Cash Invested) × 100

**Benchmark:**

- **Above 12%** — exceptional
- **8–12%** — strong
- **4–8%** — acceptable
- **0–4%** — weak (you're paying yourself less than a bond)
- **Negative** — losing money on day one

**Example, continued:** Annual cash flow = −€527 × 12 = −€6,324. Cash invested = €100,000 down + €15,000 closing ≈ €115,000.

Cash-on-cash ROI = −€6,324 ÷ €115,000 × 100 = **−5.5%**.

Three checks, three reds. The deal only makes sense as a pure appreciation bet — *or* if the rent can be raised through renovation (this is what NordInvest's [Renovation Mode](/) models directly).

## Check 4 — Break-Even Rent (10 seconds)

Break-even rent tells you how much margin you have when a tenant leaves, demands a discount, or the market softens.

**Formula:** Break-Even Rent = Monthly Mortgage + Monthly Expenses

**Benchmark:**

- **Current rent > break-even by 20%+** — strong margin of safety
- **Current rent > break-even by 5–20%** — workable
- **Current rent ≤ break-even** — no margin, walk away unless you have a thesis

**Example, continued:** Break-even rent = €2,027 + €300 = **€2,327/month**.

You're renting at €1,800. You'd need a **29% rent increase** to break even — which is precisely why this is a value-add or appreciation play, not a cashflow deal.

## Check 5 — Local Benchmark (10 seconds)

The fifth check is comparison: is the deal good *relative to the area*, not just in the abstract?

What to compare:

- **Price per m² vs. postcode average** (from Danmarks Statistik for DK, SCB for SE, SSB for NO)
- **Yield vs. local typical** (Copenhagen 3–5%, Oslo 4–6%, Stockholm 2.5–4%, Fyn 5–8%)
- **Days on market** — if 6+ months, something is wrong

If a property is priced 15%+ above the postcode average without an obvious reason, that's a red flag. If it's priced 15%+ below, that's either an opportunity or a problem — figure out which before bidding.

## The 60-Second Verdict — Three Outcomes

After running the 5 checks, you'll land in one of three buckets:

| Outcome | What it Means | What to Do |
|---|---|---|
| **All 5 pass** | Solid deal | Move forward, deeper due diligence |
| **3–4 pass** | Conditional deal | Identify the specific reason it still works (appreciation, renovation, location) |
| **0–2 pass** | Bad deal | Walk away, or restructure (lower offer, value-add plan, different financing) |

The point isn't to find a deal that passes all five — those are rare in the Nordics. The point is to know *which* checks fail and *why*, so the decision is conscious.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How much down payment do I need for a rental property in the Nordics?

In Denmark, 20–25% down is typical for an investment property (vs. 5% for owner-occupied). Sweden allows 15% for bostadsrätt purchases but lenders often want more for sublet. Norway permits 15% down on a primary, but most investors put 25% to get better rates. Higher down payment improves cash flow but hurts cash-on-cash ROI — there's a real trade-off.

### What's the difference between cash flow and ROI?

Cash flow is the monthly profit (rent minus expenses minus mortgage). ROI is what your *invested capital* earns annually as a percentage. A property can have positive cash flow but mediocre ROI (if you put too much money down), or strong ROI but tight cash flow (if you put very little down). Both numbers matter.

### Is negative cash flow ever OK on a rental?

Yes, in high-appreciation markets like Copenhagen, Oslo, or central Stockholm. If the property appreciates faster than your monthly losses accumulate, you still build wealth. The math: if you lose €500/month (€6,000/year) but the property appreciates €25,000/year, you net €19,000 in equity per year. The risk: appreciation isn't guaranteed.
