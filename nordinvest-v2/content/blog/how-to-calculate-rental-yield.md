## What Is Rental Yield?

Rental yield is the single most useful number for comparing property investments. It tells you how much annual rental income a property generates relative to its price, expressed as a percentage.

It is the first thing any serious investor calculates — and in the Nordics, where prices are high and yields are compressed, it is especially important to get right.

There are two versions you need to know: **gross yield** and **net yield**. Most listings and casual conversations refer to gross. Most real decisions should be based on net.

## Gross Rental Yield: The Formula

Gross yield is the quick, back-of-the-envelope number.

**Gross Yield = (Annual Rent ÷ Property Price) × 100**

### Worked Example

A Copenhagen apartment priced at 4,500,000 DKK, renting for 14,000 DKK per month:

- Annual rent: 14,000 × 12 = 168,000 DKK
- Gross yield: 168,000 ÷ 4,500,000 × 100 = **3.7%**

That 3.7% is typical for central Copenhagen. It tells you the property is priced for a low-yield, appreciation-driven market — useful context before you go any further.

## Net Rental Yield: The Number That Matters

Gross yield ignores costs, and costs are where Nordic investors get caught out. Net yield subtracts the real expenses of owning the property.

**Net Yield = ((Annual Rent − Annual Costs) ÷ Property Price) × 100**

Annual costs typically include:

- Property tax and municipal fees
- Building/association fees (ejerforening, sameie, samfällighet)
- Insurance
- Maintenance and repairs (budget ~1% of value per year)
- Vacancy allowance (assume a few weeks empty per year)
- Property management, if you use it

### Worked Example, Continued

Take the same Copenhagen apartment. Suppose annual costs total 38,000 DKK:

- Net annual income: 168,000 − 38,000 = 130,000 DKK
- Net yield: 130,000 ÷ 4,500,000 × 100 = **2.9%**

The gap between 3.7% and 2.9% is the difference between a number that looks fine and the number you actually live with.

## What Counts as a Good Yield in the Nordics?

Yield expectations are market-specific. Comparing a Copenhagen yield to a Lisbon yield is meaningless. As a rough Nordic guide:

| Gross Yield | Interpretation |
|---|---|
| Below 3% | Pure appreciation play — only makes sense in a strongly growing area |
| 3% – 4.5% | Typical for major Nordic cities — expect to rely on appreciation |
| 4.5% – 6% | Healthy balance of income and growth |
| Above 6% | Strong cash flow — more common in smaller cities and secondary markets |

A low yield is not automatically bad. It often signals a stable, liquid, low-risk market where the wealth comes from price appreciation rather than monthly income. The mistake is buying a low-yield property *expecting* monthly cash flow.

## Yield Is Only the Starting Point

Yield answers "how much income relative to price?" — but it does not tell you whether the deal works after a mortgage, or what happens if interest rates rise. For that you need to layer in:

- **Cash flow** — rent minus mortgage minus costs
- **Appreciation** — how fast the area's prices are growing
- **Financing** — how leverage amplifies (or threatens) your return

A property with a modest 3% yield and negative monthly cash flow can still be an excellent investment if the area appreciates steadily and you can hold for the long term. A 6% yield in a stagnant, illiquid market can be a worse one.
